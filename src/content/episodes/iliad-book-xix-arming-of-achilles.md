    en: |-
      In Book XIX, Zeus observes Achilles mourning Patroclus and, moved by pity, instructs Athene to sustain the hero with nectar and ambrosia so that hunger does not weaken him before battle. Athene descends swiftly, described in the ancient text through the oral-formulaic simile of a wide-winged falcon, and infuses the divine sustenance into Achilles' breast. As the Achaean forces arm themselves in a scene dense with formulaic imagery — shields, helms, corselets, and ashen spears whose gleam makes the earth itself appear to laugh — Achilles dons the divinely wrought armour of Hephaestus. The arming sequence follows the traditional type-scene order: greaves, corselet, sword, great shield, and finally the crested helmet, which the ancient text likens to a star. He then takes up the Pelian ash-spear, an heirloom given by Cheiron to his father Peleus, which no other Achaean can wield. Automedon and Alcimus yoke the immortal horses Xanthus and Balius, whereupon Achilles addresses them and demands they return him safely, unlike Patroclus. The horse Xanthus, granted speech by Hera, prophesies Achilles' imminent death at the hands of a god and a mortal, attributing Patroclus's death to Apollo rather than equine failure. The Erinyes silence Xanthus, and Achilles, acknowledging his fate while refusing to halt, drives his horses toward the foremost fighters.
