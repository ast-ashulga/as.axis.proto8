    en: |-
      Aias son of Telamon, overwhelmed by missiles and the will of Zeus, struggles to maintain his footing until Hector severs the head of his ashen spear with a sword-blow, leaving him armed with a useless shaft. The Trojans then hurl fire onto the ships, and flame engulfs a ship's stern, prompting Achilles to urge Patroclus to arm and lead the Myrmidons. Patroclus dons Achilles' celebrated armour — greaves, corselet, silver-studded sword, great shield, and horse-hair-crested helmet — but does not take the great Pelian ash spear, which the oral-formulaic tradition reserves as a weapon only Achilles can wield. Automedon yokes the immortal horses Xanthus and Balius, born of the West Wind and the Harpy Podarge, alongside the mortal trace-horse Pedasus. Achilles marshals the fifty-ship Myrmidon contingent, organised into five companies under Menesthius, Eudorus, Peisander, Phoenix, and Alcimedon, whose genealogies the text elaborates in characteristic epic fashion. He reminds them of their long-voiced impatience during his withdrawal, calling them now to prove that anger in battle. Before the Myrmidons advance, Achilles withdraws to his hut, draws wine from a chest placed there by silver-footed Thetis, and prays to Zeus of Dodona, asking that glory accompany Patroclus and that he return unharmed from the fighting. Zeus grants the first part of the prayer but withholds the second, a narrative signal whose ironic weight the poem makes explicit. The Myrmidons pour from the ships in a simile comparing them first to ravening wolves gorged on a stag and then to roadside wasps stirred from their nests, both similes functioning as oral-formulaic type-scenes emphasising collective fury. The Trojans, glimpsing Patroclus in Achilles' armour, mistake him for Achilles returned and their ranks are shaken. Patroclus drives his spear into the crowd near the stern of Protesilaus's ship, kills the Paeonian leader Pyraechmes, and quenches the fire. A sustained aristeia follows in which Patroclus and allied Danaan chieftains — including Menelaus, Antilochus, Thrasymedes, Meriones, and Idomeneus — cut down a series of named Trojan warriors in rapid succession. The Trojans break and flee back across the trench in disorder, many chariots splintering their poles in the crossing, while Patroclus pursues relentlessly, slaying men as they tumble from their cars. A simile comparing the rout to Zeus pouring rain upon a black earth in harvest-time, a punishment for unjust judges, frames the destruction with cosmic-judicial weight characteristic of the ancient text. Hemming the survivors between the ships, the river, and the city wall, Patroclus kills Pronous, Thestor, Erylaus, and a catalogue of others, with the fish-hook simile marking the death of Thestor as a vivid oral-formulaic embellishment.
