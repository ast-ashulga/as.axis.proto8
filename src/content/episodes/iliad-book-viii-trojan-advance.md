    en: |-
      The Achaean lines collapse under divine pressure in this segment of Book VIII, as Idomeneus, Agamemnon, and both Aiantes abandon the field, leaving only Nestor stranded when his horse is fatally struck by an arrow from Paris. Diomedes alone stands firm, rescuing the aged Nestor and taking him aboard his chariot to drive against Hector. Diomedes casts his spear at Hector but kills instead his charioteer Eniopeus, son of Thebaeus, a formulaic aristeia-moment that temporarily disrupts the Trojan advance. Zeus intervenes decisively, hurling a thunderbolt before Diomedes' horses, and Nestor counsels retreat, acknowledging that divine will overrides human valour. Diomedes withdraws reluctantly, tortured by the prospect of Hector's future boasts, a concern rooted in the poem's deep logic of timē and kleos. Hector then addresses his horses by name — Xanthus, Podargus, Aethon, and Lampus — in a passage that invokes Andromache's care for them, linking domestic tenderness to the ferocity of the Trojan advance.
