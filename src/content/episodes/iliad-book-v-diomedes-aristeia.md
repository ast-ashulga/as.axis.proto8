    en: |-
      Pallas Athene ignites divine fire upon the helm and shield of Diomedes, son of Tydeus, marking him out as pre-eminent among the Argives and inaugurating his aristeia — the extended passage of heroic excellence that structures much of Book V. He first encounters the sons of Dares, a priest of Hephaestus: Phegeus casts his spear and misses, but Diomedes kills him, while Idaeus is rescued by Hephaestus himself, enveloped in darkness to spare the aged priest complete devastation. Athene removes Ares from the battlefield, after which a sequence of Achaean killings unfolds — Agamemnon, Idomeneus, Menelaus, Meriones, Meges, and Eurypylus each slay named Trojans in rapid succession, a structural feature of the oral-formulaic tradition known as a mass-slaughter catalogue. Diomedes storms across the plain compared in an extended simile to a winter torrent overwhelming all embankments, his allegiance momentarily indistinguishable from either side. Pandarus, son of Lycaon, wounds him with an arrow through the shoulder-plate; Sthenelus withdraws the arrow, and Diomedes prays to Athene, invoking his father Tydeus as a model of dauntless courage. Athene responds by clearing a divine mist from his eyes, granting him the capacity to distinguish gods from mortals in battle, and instructs him to wound Aphrodite if she enters the fight but to avoid all other immortals — a restriction that the subsequent narrative will dramatically test. Diomedes then slays a further series of Trojans, including two sons of Priam, with the simile of a lion breaking the neck of cattle underscoring the ferocity — a type-scene comparison characteristic of the ancient Greek epic tradition.
