    en: |-
      Following the Trojan lament, Achilles refuses to let the Myrmidons disperse, leading them in a ritual triple circuit of Patroclus's corpse with chariots and horses, a mourning rite rooted in the oral-formulaic tradition's established type-scenes of funerary honour. Achilles addresses Patroclus directly, invoking the promises he has fulfilled — the degradation of Hector's body and the vow to sacrifice twelve Trojan captives before the pyre — framing vengeance as a form of devotion within the poem's agonistic logic of timē and kleos. He refuses to wash the blood from his body until Patroclus is properly cremated, swearing an oath before Zeus. Sleeping on the shore, he is visited by the psychē of Patroclus, which appears in perfect likeness and pleads urgently for burial so that it may pass through the gates of Hades, currently barred from crossing the River. The ghost also requests that their bones be interred together in a single golden urn, recalling their shared childhood at Peleus's court, and foretells Achilles' own imminent death beneath the walls of Troy. When Achilles reaches out to embrace the spirit, it dissolves like vapour beneath the earth, prompting his famous reflection that even in Hades something of the self persists, though without full understanding. Dawn rises as Achilles reports the vision to the assembled warriors, and communal lamentation resumes.
