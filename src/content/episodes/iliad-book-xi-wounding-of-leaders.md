      В одиннадцатой книге «Илиады» разворачивается серия поединков, ставших центральными для всего сказания о ранениях вождей. Агамемнон сражает Ификодама, сына Антенора, юношу, недавно взявшего жену и покинувшего брачный покой ради войны, и снимает с него доспехи. Старший брат павшего, Коон, наносит Агамемнону копьём удар в руку, однако сам гибнет от его же меча. Рана начинает нестерпимо жечь — устойчивый образ, уподобляющий боль от раны муке роженицы, — и царь вынужден покинуть поле боя на колеснице. Узнав об отступлении Агамемнона, Гектор бросается вперёд и в стремительной аристии истребляет множество данайских вождей. Диомед и Одиссей пытаются сдержать натиск: Диомед метает копьё в Гектора и оглушает его ударом по шлему, но Аполлон спасает троянского героя. Засевший в засаде Парис пронзает Диомеду ступню стрелой, и тот тоже вынужден отступить; устами Диомеда традиция выражает презрение к луку как оружию недостойного бойца. Оставшийся в одиночестве Одиссей отражает натиск окруживших его троянцев, однако Сок, сын Гиппаса, пронзает ему бок копьём; на помощь приходят Менелай и Аякс, рассеивающие врагов. Аякс сдерживает троянский напор подобно льву, отогнанному от стада, — один из развёрнутых гомеровских симилей, закреплённых в устно-формульной традиции. Наконец, Парис ранит стрелой Махаона, врача и сына Асклепия, что побуждает Идоменея срочно отправить его с Нестором к кораблям: устойчивая формула традиции подчёркивает, что один врач стоит многих воинов.
    en: |-
      The poet opens this segment of Book XI with a formal invocation of the Muses, a traditional oral-formulaic device, before recounting how Iphidamas, son of Antenor, is the first to face Agamemnon in single combat. Iphidamas, raised in Thrace by his maternal grandfather Cisseus and newly wed, left his bride to fight at Troy with twelve ships, which he left at Percote before marching on to Ilios. Agamemnon misses with his spear but kills Iphidamas with a sword-stroke to the neck after the young warrior's spear-thrust bends against the silver of Agamemnon's belt, and he strips the body of its armour. Coön, Iphidamas's elder brother, then wounds Agamemnon in the arm with a spear-thrust, but Agamemnon kills Coön in turn and decapitates him over his fallen brother's body, fulfilling what the text frames as the destined fate of both sons of Antenor. Agamemnon continues fighting while his wound bleeds warm, but when the blood dries and sharp pain overtakes him—compared in an oral-formulaic simile to the anguish of a woman in labour sent by the Eileithyiai—he mounts his chariot and withdraws, urging the Danaans to defend the ships. Hector, observing Agamemnon's departure, rallies the Trojans with a battle-cry and drives forward like a storm upon the sea, slaying a catalogue of Danaan leaders and pressing the Achaeans toward their ships. Odysseus and Diomedes rally together; though Diomedes acknowledges that Zeus favours the Trojans, he strikes Thymbraeus from his chariot while Odysseus kills Molion, and the two press through the Trojan ranks like boars among hounds. Diomedes hurls his spear at Hector and strikes his helmet, stunning him momentarily, but Apollo's gift of the triple-crested helm deflects the bronze, and Hector recovers and escapes in his chariot. While Diomedes strips the armour of Agastrophus, Paris shoots him through the flat of the right foot with an arrow from ambush behind the tomb of Ilus, and though the wound does not end Diomedes' life it forces him to withdraw from battle. Odysseus is left alone when his comrades flee; the text preserves a soliloquy in which he debates whether to stand or retreat, a brief interior monologue consistent with the poem's oral-formulaic type-scene of the isolated hero. Surrounded by Trojans, Odysseus fights off multiple warriors—including Deïopites, Thoön, Eunomus, and Chersidamas—before Socus, son of Hippasus, spears him through his shield and corselet, tearing the flesh of his side, though Athene prevents a fatal wound. Odysseus kills Socus with a return thrust as he flees, but the drawn spear causes his blood to flow, drawing the Trojans upon him until Menelaus and Ajax hear his triple war-shout and fight through the throng to rescue him. Ajax then ranges across the plain in a torrent simile, slaying many Trojans, while on the left flank Hector engages Nestor and Idomeneus near the Scamander. Paris wounds the physician Machaon, son of Asclepius, with a three-barbed arrow in the right shoulder; Idomeneus urges Nestor to carry him swiftly to the ships, declaring in a formulaic expression that a physician is worth many men. Cebriones alerts Hector to Ajax's rout of the Trojans on the right wing, and Hector drives there, though he carefully avoids direct engagement with Ajax. Zeus then causes Ajax to retreat step by step in a famous double simile comparing him first to a lion driven from a cattle-fold and then to a stubborn ass slowly expelled from a cornfield by boys. Finally, Eurypylus comes to Ajax's aid, kills Apisaon, but is himself wounded in the thigh by an arrow from Paris; he calls on the Argives to stand firm around Ajax before withdrawing into the throng, concluding this sustained sequence of Achaean commanders wounded or forced from the field.
