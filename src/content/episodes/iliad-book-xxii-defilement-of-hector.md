    en: |-
      After slaying Hector, Achilles withdraws his spear and strips the blood-stained armour from the corpse, while the assembled Achaeans gather to gaze upon Hector's imposing form and take turns wounding the body, with one soldier's taunt preserved in the oral-formulaic tradition recording that Hector is "softer to handle now than when he burned the ships". Achilles then addresses the Argive leaders, acknowledging divine assistance in the victory and proposing a reconnaissance against Troy, before catching himself and declaring that Patroclus, still unburied by the ships, remains his paramount concern. He then devises a deliberate defilement of Hector's body, piercing the tendons between heel and ankle, threading ox-hide thongs through the wounds, and dragging the corpse behind his chariot so that Hector's dark hair trails in the dust — a scene the poem frames as Zeus delivering Hector to suffer dishonour in his own native land. On the walls, Hecuba tears her hair and casts away her veil, Priam groans aloud and must be physically restrained from rushing to the Achaean ships, and he appeals by name to each bystander, invoking his shared fatherhood with Peleus in a plea for pity. Hecuba's lament recalls how Hector was honoured by the Trojans as a god throughout his life. Andromache, still ignorant of her husband's fate and preparing a bath for his return — the dramatic irony underscored by the formulaic epithet "flashing-eyed Athene" as the agent of Hector's destruction — hears the wailing from the wall and collapses, scattering the ornaments Aphrodite had given her on her wedding day. Reviving, she delivers a sustained lament for Hector, for her son Astyanax's fatherless future, and for Hector's body left to dogs and worms far from the ritual honours of burial, closing with a vow to burn his fine garments as the only tribute now available to her.
