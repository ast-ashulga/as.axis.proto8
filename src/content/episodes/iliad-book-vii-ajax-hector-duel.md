    en: |-
      In Book VII of the Iliad, Hector and Paris re-enter the fighting, their arrival compared through an oral-formulaic simile to a wind that relieves exhausted sailors. Hector kills Eioneus and Paris's companion Glaucus wounds Iphinous before the gods intervene. Athena descends from Olympus to aid the Achaeans, but Apollo intercepts her at the oak tree and proposes that the battle be suspended through a formal single combat, a proposal she accepts. The seer Helenus perceives this divine plan and urges Hector to challenge the Achaeans' best warrior. Hector addresses both armies and issues his challenge, declaring that if he falls his opponent may keep his armour, while his own body must be returned for proper burial rites, and that the victor's barrow will be remembered by future seafarers — a passage that foregrounds the oral-formulaic concern with kleos and its transmission. The Achaeans are initially silent with shame and fear until Menelaus volunteers, though Agamemnon restrains him on the grounds that even Achilles dreads Hector. Nestor rebukes the assembled chiefs with a long reminiscence about his own youthful duel against Ereuthalion, invoking the same agonistic framework of timē and martial honour. Nine chieftains then volunteer, and lots cast in Agamemnon's helmet select Ajax son of Telamon. Ajax advances carrying his great seven-layered ox-hide shield, itself identified by the formulaic epithet "like a city wall," and the Trojans tremble while Hector, bound by his own challenge, cannot withdraw. The two warriors exchange spear casts and stone throws in an evenly matched combat until Ajax's great stone staggers Hector, at which point Apollo raises him. Heralds Talthybius and Idaeus intervene to separate the combatants at nightfall, and the duel ends with an exchange of gifts: Hector gives his silver-studded sword and Ajax gives his crimson-dyed belt. Both warriors return to their respective camps, where the Achaeans honour Ajax at a feast, and Agamemnon, characterised by the formulaic epithet "wide-ruling," awards him the choice cut of the sacrifice.
