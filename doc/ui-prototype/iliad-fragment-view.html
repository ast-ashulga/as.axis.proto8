THE RANSOM OF HECTOR

Book XXIV of the Iliad

Priam, old king of Troy, has crossed enemy lines alone at night. Hermes, sent by Zeus, appeared to him disguised as a young Myrmidon soldier and guided him past the Greek sentries and through the sleeping camp to Achilles' hut. Priam arrives after Achilles has eaten; his two companions sit apart. Priam enters unnoticed and kneels before Achilles.

He clasps Achilles' knees and kisses his hands — the terrible man-slaying hands that killed Hector and so many of Priam's other sons. He begs Achilles to think of his own father Peleus, who is old and alone and will never see his son again. He is, Priam says, in a worse position than Peleus: he had the best sons in Troy and has lost them all. He asks only for Hector's body, to bury it properly.

Achilles is overcome. He weeps for his own father and for Patroclus. Priam weeps for Hector. The two men weep together, side by side, in the same hut, for their separate dead. Neither can stop the other. Neither tries. When the weeping finally subsides, Achilles raises Priam by his hand and speaks to him with something like gentleness: the gods have spun this thread for mortals, he says — to live in grief — while the gods themselves have no sorrows. He agrees to return the body. He instructs his companions to prepare it: wash it, anoint it, wrap it, so that Priam does not see the wounds and renew his grief before he can get away.

Achilles and Priam share a meal. Priam marvels at Achilles; Achilles marvels at Priam. Achilles promises eleven days of truce for the funeral rites — he will hold the Greeks back. Before dawn, Hermes wakes Priam and leads him out of the camp. Priam crosses the plain with Hector's body on the cart and reaches Troy as the sun rises. The poem's last word is the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses.

MANUSCRIPT STATUS

Book XXIV survives in all major manuscript traditions of the Iliad. The Venetus A (Marcianus graecus 454, 10th century CE) is the most important witness: it preserves not only the text but extensive marginal scholia recording the editorial work of the Alexandrian scholars, including Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium, and Aristarchus. The book is attested in dozens of Roman-era papyri from Egypt, several of which preserve lines not found in the medieval manuscripts.

TEXTUAL HISTORY

Aristarchus of Samothrace (c. 216–144 BCE) athetized (bracketed as potentially spurious) approximately 12 lines in Book XXIV, including portions of the opening divine assembly (lines 25–30) where the gods debate Hector's treatment. His reasons are recorded in the Venetus A scholia: some lines seemed inconsistent with earlier divine characterizations, others appeared metrically anomalous. Subsequent editors have largely restored these lines, but the athetesis marks an important moment in the transmission history and indicates that ancient readers were already uncertain about the text's edges.

SCHOLARLY DEBATE: THE ENDING OF THE ILIAD

Whether Book XXIV was the original ending of the poem — or whether a different ending existed in earlier oral versions — remains actively debated. Cedric Whitman (1958) argued that the poem's geometric ring-composition structure requires Book XXIV as its inevitable close: the symmetrical pattern of the poem demands that what begins with Achilles' rage and Chryses' ransom request end with another ransom and another old father. M.L. West (2011) argued that the final scene is compositionally anomalous in several respects and may reflect late editorial addition or expansion. The current majority position holds Book XXIV as integral to the poem's structure and meaning; the debate hinges on oral composition theory and the weight given to manuscript evidence versus structural argument.

PROPP
Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928)

Vladimir Propp identified 31 narrative "functions" — actions that advance the story — recurring in Russian folktales in a consistent sequence. Later scholars applied the framework to myth and epic traditions worldwide. The numbers (PROPP-8, PROPP-19, etc.) are the function index from Propp's original 1928 classification.

TMI (Thompson Motif Index)
Thompson Motif Index (1955–1958)

Stith Thompson's six-volume index classifies recurring narrative motifs across world folklore and mythology. Each motif has an alphanumeric code. P-codes cover social relations and customs; T-codes cover sex, love, and the emotions.

TMI P325 · Guest and host

Xenia — the Greek guest-friendship code — is observed between enemies. Achilles provides Priam with a seat, a shared meal, and safe passage home. The truce is a xenia gesture that overrides the war context entirely. Thompson P325 covers guest receives hospitality.

Mourning for dead

TMI T211.9 · Mourning for dead

Mutual mourning of enemies is documented as a rare motif. Thompson T211.9 covers ritualized mourning behaviors; this scene is among its most formally elaborate instances in world literature: two men who are enemies weeping together for separate losses, neither able to comfort the other.
