About structural resonances

A structural resonance is not a claim that one tradition borrowed from another. It is a documented observation that two or more traditions share a recognisable narrative shape — a similar sequence of events, a similar human situation, a similar structural function for a character or event.

All parallels on this page have been confirmed by a scholar after review. Computationally-detected candidate parallels are never shown publicly until a scholar has reviewed and confirmed them. The type labels ("shared human condition", "shared narrative shape", "shared social pattern") describe the nature of the resonance — they are not claims about historical contact or origin.

The Great Flood

Gilgamesh (Tablet XI) ↔ Genesis 6–9 (KJV)

Both traditions depict a divine judgment by flood, a chosen survivor warned through an indirect channel, and birds sent to find dry land. The structure is shared with remarkable precision across traditions separated by culture and century. What the survivor receives diverges entirely: immortality in Gilgamesh, covenant in Genesis.

Type: Shared human condition — not a shared source

Traditions: Mesopotamian · Hebrew / Biblical

Grief and the Hero

Iliad (Book XXIV) ↔ Epic of Gilgamesh (Tablet VIII)

Both heroes lose their closest companion — Patroclus for Achilles, Enkidu for Gilgamesh. Both are left to confront mortality through the loss. The companion functions as the hero's alter-ego; the companion's death is the hinge on which heroic identity turns. The traditions diverge in what the hero does with that grief: Achilles accepts and restores relation; Gilgamesh refuses and seeks escape.

Type: Structural resonance — not a shared source

Traditions: Greek · Mesopotamian
