GREEK

The Iliad

c. 8th century BCE (oral tradition c. 12th–9th c. BCE)  ·  Attributed to Homer  ·  Ancient Greek  ·  Dactylic hexameter

The Iliad is not a story about the Trojan War. It is a story about a few weeks within the war's tenth year, and it is about rage — the rage of Achilles, son of Peleus and the sea-goddess Thetis, and what that rage costs him, his enemies, and the people he loves. The poem begins with a quarrel over a captive woman between Achilles and Agamemnon, the commander of the Greek forces. Achilles withdraws from battle. His withdrawal nearly destroys the Greek cause. His return destroys Hector, prince of Troy, and ends with Achilles giving Hector's body back to Priam, his father, in the final act of the poem's enormous moral arc.

The text survives in approximately 1,800 medieval manuscripts — a transmission record unmatched in antiquity. The standard critical edition follows the work of the Alexandrian scholars of the 3rd–2nd century BCE, particularly Aristarchus of Samothrace, who produced the most influential recensio. The poem was composed in oral tradition before writing, probably first committed to text in the late 8th century BCE; the received text reflects centuries of performance variation before stabilization. 24 books. Approximately 15,693 lines.

BOOK I

The Wrath

Chryses the priest comes to ransom his daughter; Agamemnon refuses and insults him. Apollo sends plague. Achilles calls an assembly; the quarrel erupts; Achilles withdraws from battle and prays to Thetis. Zeus agrees to honour Achilles by letting the Trojans prevail.

BOOK VI

Farewell

Hector returns to Troy to arrange sacrifices. He finds Andromache on the tower of the Scaean Gate. Their farewell: Hector reaches for Astyanax, who recoils from the horsehair plume of his father's helmet. The child frightened by his own father's war-face is the most-remembered image in the poem.

BOOK IX

Embassy

With the Greek camp under pressure, Agamemnon sends Phoenix, Odysseus, and Ajax to Achilles with gifts and an apology. Achilles refuses — but he wavers. His speech on mortality presents two fates: short glorious life or long obscurity. He chooses neither yet.

BOOK XVI

Patroclus

Achilles allows Patroclus to borrow his armor and lead the Myrmidons. Patroclus drives back the Trojans but defies Achilles' instructions and presses too far. Apollo strips him of his armor. Euphorbus wounds him first; Hector kills him. Achilles does not yet know.

BOOK XXII

Hector

Hector waits alone outside Troy for Achilles while Priam and Hecuba beg from the walls. Hector resolves to fight, then turns and runs — three times around the city walls. Athena deceives him into stopping. He dies asking for his body to be returned; Achilles refuses. The body is dragged.

The Ransom

Zeus intervenes; Priam crosses enemy lines; both men weep for their dead; Hector's body is returned. The poem ends not with victory but with grief shared between enemies and a truce observed in its honour.
